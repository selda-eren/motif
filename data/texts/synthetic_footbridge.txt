Harlow lies on both banks of the river Lenn, and for most of its history the two halves of the town have been joined by a single wooden footbridge. The bridge was built in 1912 by the carpenters' guild of the west bank and the masons' guild of the east bank, working together for the first time after decades of rivalry. There is no other crossing for twenty kilometres in either direction.

The weekly market is held every Saturday in the square on the east bank. The vegetable growers of the west bank carry their produce over on handcarts at dawn, and for most of these households the Saturday stalls bring in more than half of their yearly income.

Since 1920 the town seal has shown the footbridge beneath two clasped hands. The council's own guide to the seal explains that the image stands for the union of the two banks and for the reconciliation of the guilds that built it.

The railings were repainted dark green in 2019, after a public vote in which green narrowly defeated a proposal to restore the original white. A council survey published in 2021 reported that the footbridge had been inspected every year and was structurally sound.

The only school in Harlow stands on the east bank, near the church. About ninety children from the west bank walk to it each morning, and the school timetable begins at a quarter past eight so that they can cross after the market carts have passed. Teachers say that the walk is the part of the day the children talk about most.

In 2022 the west-bank growers formed a cooperative and built a shared cold store beside their fields. It keeps vegetables fresh through the week, so that everything picked between Sunday and Friday can still be sold at the Saturday stalls. The cooperative borrowed heavily to build it and expected to repay the loan within six years from market sales.

The town's only doctor keeps her surgery on the west bank, in the old customs house. Patients from the east bank cross to see her on weekday mornings, and on Thursdays she holds a clinic for the elderly, many of whom are pushed over the footbridge in wheelchairs by relatives or neighbours. She has said that she chose the customs house because it is a short walk from the bridge, so that both halves of the town could reach her easily.

The council has also begun planning a festival for the bridge's centenary of restoration, due in 2025, with lanterns hung along the green railings and a procession from one bank to the other. A local poet has written verses for the occasion, ending with the line "two banks, one step". The festival committee has already ordered three hundred paper lanterns and asked both guilds to lead the procession together, as their founders did when the bridge first opened.

The river itself has changed little. Trout are still caught below the weir, and the library on the east bank keeps a record of the largest fish landed each summer, a tradition that dates from the nineteenth century. The library also lends fishing rods to children during the school holidays, and its reading room holds the town's collection of old maps and parish registers.

Post for the whole town is sorted at the post office on the east bank. Twice a day the postman carries the letters for the west bank across in a canvas bag, and the post office has never needed a second branch because, as the postmaster likes to say, the bridge is only four minutes long. Parcels too large for the bag are wheeled over on a trolley that the post office bought for the purpose in the 1960s and still uses.

In March 2024, after a week of heavy rain, the Lenn rose higher than at any time in living memory. Shortly before midnight on the fourteenth, the flood carried the footbridge away. By morning only the stone footings remained on either bank, and the timbers were later found scattered for several kilometres downstream.

An engineering firm was asked to report on what had happened. Its report found that the footbridge had not been inspected at all since 1998, and that rot in the main beams had gone unnoticed for many years. The firm concluded that a sound bridge would probably have survived the flood.

The council has said that a new crossing cannot be built in less than three years. Until then, the nearest way from one bank to the other is the road bridge twenty kilometres to the north.
